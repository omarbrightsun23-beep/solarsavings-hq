Solar Panel Cost in 2026: Price per Watt by System Size (6/8/10/12 kW) — and How to Check Any Quote

Three public benchmarks side by side, totals for 6, 8, 10 and 12 kW systems, and a three-step check to place any quote against the published evidence.

Price: $2.60–$3.60/W
8 kW: $20,960–$28,800
Benchmarks: 3 Public
Federal Credit: $0 in 2026

Quick answer: A home solar system in 2026 costs roughly $2.60 to $3.60 per watt before incentives, depending on which public benchmark you use — and in 2026 there is no federal tax credit for a homeowner who buys, because §25D expired on December 31, 2025, so for most buyers the benchmark price is the price. For the most common size, 8 kW, that is $20,960 at EnergySage's marketplace average ($2.62/W) or $28,800 at Berkeley Lab's median of what homeowners actually paid ($3.60/W). The two benchmarks disagree by about $1 a watt because they measure different things — this article puts them side by side, by system size, and shows you how to place your quote against them.

The Benchmarks

The three public benchmarks, side by side

There is no official price of solar. There are three public numbers that honest quoting can be checked against, and they do not agree — which is useful, because together they form a range a fair quote should fall inside.

Benchmark (checked Nov 2026)What it actually measuresPrice per watt
EnergySage marketplace, H1 2026The average of competing quotes offered through EnergySage's marketplace — shoppers comparing several installers$2.57/W median overall; $2.62/W for solar-only systems
NLR (NREL) modeled benchmark, Q1 2025A modeled "market price" for a standard residential system, built up from component and labour costs$2.95/W (modeled range roughly $2.32–$3.25/W)
Berkeley Lab, 2025 installs (2026 Data Update)The median of what homeowners actually paid for systems installed in 2025, recorded across ~95% of the US market$3.60/W (2025 dollars)

Why the spread? EnergySage sees quotes from installers competing for shoppers who are actively comparing prices — the sharp end of the market. Berkeley Lab sees completed installations, including expensive installers, complex roofs, and loan-financed systems whose prices carry lender fees. NREL's modeled figure sits between them, as a build-up of what a system should cost. None of the three is "the price." A quote below EnergySage's average is possible (simple roof, hungry installer, cheap state). A quote above Berkeley Lab's median needs an explanation. A quote presented with no benchmark at all — just a monthly payment — is a quote that doesn't want to be checked.

One more Berkeley Lab split that explains a lot of high quotes: among 2025 installations, cash buyers paid a median of $3.00/W and loan-financed buyers a median of $4.50/W. The hardware is the same; financed prices commonly carry lender "dealer fees" rolled into the system price. If you are financing, ask for the cash price of the identical system in writing — the difference is the cost of the loan before interest.

Cost by System Size

Cost by system size: 6, 8, 10 and 12 kW

Price per watt falls slightly as systems get bigger, because fixed costs — permitting, design, the crew's day on your roof — spread across more panels. Here are the fetched EnergySage $/W figures by size, with the totals at both the EnergySage average and the Berkeley Lab all-systems median:

EnergySage size-specific $/W from its H1 2026 marketplace data; Berkeley Lab and NLR are single all-size benchmarks applied across sizes for comparison — Berkeley Lab's own size-band medians vary, so treat the Berkeley column as "at the overall median," not as a size-specific statistic.

How big are these systems in practice? Berkeley Lab puts the median US home system installed in 2025 at 7.7 kW, with most systems between 5 and 12 kW. As a rough orientation only: at a national-average-style yield of about 1,400 kWh per kW per year, a 6 kW system produces roughly 8,400 kWh a year, an 8 kW about 11,400 kWh, a 10 kW about 14,000 kWh, and a 12 kW about 16,800 kWh. Your roof's actual yield — from NREL's free PVWatts calculator, using your address, tilt and direction — is the only version of that number worth planning on; yields across US population centres run roughly 1,100–1,900 kWh per kW.

And the credit that is no longer in these numbers: every total above is a gross price, and for a system installed in 2026 and bought by the homeowner, gross is what you pay. Until December 31, 2025, the §25D federal credit would have returned 30% — $6,288 on the $20,960 system. It expired, with no grandfather clause for contracts signed earlier (the IRS counts the expenditure when installation is completed). Some states still pay their own incentives — New York's state credit, for example, is 25% of cost up to $5,000 — and those genuinely reduce the totals above for eligible buyers. Check your state on DSIRE (dsireusa.org) and on your state's page on this site. But if a 2026 quote shows a federal 30% line, that quote is wrong — our solar quote red-flag checklist walks through that and nine other checks.

What a Watt Buys

What a watt actually buys

Berkeley Lab's analysis attributes roughly 80% of a residential system's installed price to soft costs and balance-of-system — labour, permitting, design, customer acquisition, overhead and margin — rather than to the panels themselves. That single fact explains most of what confuses buyers:

  • Panel prices falling does not make installed prices fall at the same speed. The panel is a minority of your invoice.
  • Two quotes for identical panels can differ by $1/W because the difference is in the companies, not the hardware — their acquisition costs, overheads, warranty reserves and margin.
  • Financing changes the hardware price itself (the cash $3.00/W vs loan $4.50/W medians above), because dealer fees are priced into the system, not shown as loan costs.

It also explains the installer spread Berkeley Lab found: across the top 100 US residential installers in 2025, median prices ran from about $2.4/W to $6.3/W. The same product, installed on similar roofs, at two-and-a-half times the price. Getting three quotes is not a formality in this market; it is most of the game.

Price Factors

What moves your price off the benchmarks

Legitimate reasons a real quote lands above the EnergySage average:

  • A battery. Paired solar-plus-storage systems recorded a Berkeley Lab median of $4.90/W of solar against $3.60/W for solar alone — and EnergySage put battery pricing at about $1,130 per kWh of storage in H1 2026. If a quote includes a battery, separate its cost before comparing $/W, or the comparison is meaningless.
  • Roof reality. Steep pitch, tile or slate, multiple small roof planes, or a roof that needs replacing first.
  • Electrical work. A main-panel upgrade or a long trench run to a detached structure.
  • Your state. EnergySage's state averages in its 2026 data run from about $2.12/W in Florida to $3.75/W in Alabama, with Texas and Arizona near $2.25/W and Hawaii near $3.44/W. A quote should be judged against your state's range first and the national range second.
  • How you pay. Cash at the low end; a loan's dealer fee can add more than any roof complication (see the $3.00 vs $4.50 medians).

Reasons that are not legitimate: a "premium" label with no named panel model, a price that expires today, or a quote that won't state its $/W at all.

Payback Illustration

What the system is worth: a payback illustration, assumptions shown

Cost is only half the question. Here is a deliberately simple illustration — an estimate, not a prediction, with every assumption visible so you can swap in your own:

  • System: 8 kW at the EnergySage average — $20,960, bought for cash, no state incentive.
  • Production: about 11,400 kWh in year one (≈1,400 kWh per kW — a mid-range US yield; check yours in PVWatts).
  • Electricity price: the US average residential rate, 18.16¢/kWh (EIA, August 2026).
  • Assumption that flatters the result: every kilowatt-hour offsets power bought at the full retail rate. Under low export rates (California's NEM 3.0 is the extreme case), exported power is worth far less and payback stretches.

On those assumptions the system avoids about $2,070 of purchases in year one, for a simple payback of roughly 10 years — before any electricity-price rises (which shorten it) and before panel degradation and any maintenance (which lengthen it). At the Berkeley Lab median price of $28,800 for the same production, the same maths gives roughly 14 years. Same panels, same sun, same rate — the price you pay moves the payback by four years. That is why the quote check below matters more than any other part of going solar in 2026.

Quote Check

How to check any quote in three steps

  1. Get the $/W. Total price before incentives ÷ system size in watts. (A $26,500 quote for 9 kW is 26,500 ÷ 9,000 = $2.94/W.) If the quote won't give you a total price and a wattage, stop there.
  2. Place it against the three benchmarks. Below ~$2.60/W: competitive nationally — check your state's range to be sure. Between ~$2.60 and ~$3.60/W: inside the published evidence; judge the details. Above ~$3.60/W: require an itemised, written explanation (battery? roof? panel upgrade?) — and if you're financing, demand the cash price too, to expose any dealer fee.
  3. Check the production claim. Run your address through PVWatts and compare. A claim more than ~10% above PVWatts for the same roof inflates every savings figure in the proposal by the same amount.

Then get two more quotes and run all three through the same steps. For the full list of traps — the expired-credit line, escalators, pressure deadlines, vague equipment — see the solar quote red-flag checklist.
