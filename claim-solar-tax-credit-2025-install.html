Installed Solar in 2025? How to Claim the 30% Federal Tax Credit on Your 2026 Taxes

The Residential Clean Energy Credit ended for new installations on December 31, 2025 — but a system completed in 2025 still claims the full 30% on Form 5695. Here is exactly how, line by line.

Credit: 30% (IRC § 25D)
Install Deadline: Dec 31, 2025
Claim Form: IRS 5695, Part I
Status: Claimable for 2025 Installs

The short answer: yes, the credit is still yours. The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) ended on December 31, 2025 — but only for new installations. If your system's installation was completed in 2025, you claim the full 30% on the tax return for 2025, using IRS Form 5695, Part I. The IRS is explicit about this: the credit "equals 30% of the costs of new, qualified clean energy property for your home installed anytime from 2022 through December 31, 2025." What changed is that it "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025."

That one date — when your installation was completed, not when you signed or paid — decides everything. This guide walks through who qualifies, what the 30% actually covers, the Form 5695 mechanics line by line, what happens if your tax bill is too small to absorb the credit, and the mistakes that cost people the whole amount.

A note on timing, because most pages get this wrong. The credit for a 2025 installation belongs on your 2025 tax return — the return most people file in early 2026. If you already filed that return and left the credit off, you can amend it. If your 2025 tax bill was too small to use the whole credit, the leftover amount moves onto your 2026 return as a carryforward. Either way, nothing about a completed 2025 installation expired on January 1, 2026. What expired was the ability to start a new claim for a system installed in 2026 or later.

Last updated: November 2026. This article is general information about a federal tax credit, not tax advice for your specific situation — see the note at the end.

The Law Changed

What changed on December 31, 2025 (and why so many pages are stale)

The 30% credit was originally scheduled to run at that rate through 2032. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) cut it short: for the Residential Clean Energy Credit, the IRS states that the credit "will not be allowed for any expenditures made after December 31, 2025" (IRS Fact Sheet FS-2025-05).

Most solar pages on the internet were written before that law passed and still subtract 30% from the cost of a system you would install today. That maths is now wrong for new installations. It is still exactly right for one group of people: homeowners whose installations were completed in 2025. If that is you, ignore every headline saying "the solar tax credit is gone." For your system, it isn't — you just have to claim it correctly.

One separate point, so it doesn't cause confusion: if you lease your system or buy its power under a PPA, you never claimed this credit in the first place — you don't own the equipment. The leasing company claims a business credit instead (the commercial credit, Section 48E, has a later end date). Everything below is for people who own their system, whether they paid cash or financed it.

Eligibility

Who qualifies: the checklist

You can claim the credit for a 2025 installation if all of the following are true:

  • ☐ Installation was completed in 2025. The IRS treats an expenditure as made "when the original installation of the item is completed" — more on this below, because it is the single biggest trap.
  • ☐ You own the system. Bought outright or financed in your name. Leased systems and PPAs don't qualify for the homeowner credit (per the Department of Energy's homeowner guide).
  • ☐ The home is in the United States and you live in it. The IRS allows the credit for your main home "whether you own or rent it" — a renter who paid for the installation can claim; a landlord who doesn't live in the home cannot. Solar electric property can also qualify at a second home you live in part-time and don't rent out (the Form 5695 instructions note that for solar electric property, "the home doesn't have to be your main home").
  • ☐ The equipment was new. "Used (previously owned) clean energy property is not eligible," and the credit can only be claimed on the original installation.
  • ☐ The property isn't purely for business. If you use your home partly for business: business use up to 20% still gets the full credit; above 20%, only the non-business share of the costs counts.
  • ☐ You have federal tax liability — not to qualify, but to use the credit this year. The credit is nonrefundable (explained below); anything you can't use carries forward.

Two things that are not requirements, because people ask constantly: there is no income limit on this credit, and there is no annual or lifetime dollar cap on the amount — the IRS's only dollar limit is on fuel cell property, which almost no home solar installation involves.

Qualified Costs

What the 30% actually covers

The credit is 30% of your qualified costs, and the cost base is broader than the panels alone. Per the IRS, qualified expenses include:

  • Solar electric panels and the equipment that goes with them (the IRS's cost lines cover the property itself; the Department of Energy's homeowner guide spells out the usual components — mounting equipment, inverter, wiring).
  • Battery storage of at least 3 kWh. Since 2023, battery storage technology is its own qualifying category: a battery with a capacity of at least 3 kilowatt hours, installed in connection with your home, goes on its own line of Form 5695. It does not have to be installed on the same day as new panels.
  • Labour. "Labor costs for onsite preparation, assembly or original installation of the property and for piping or wiring to connect it to the home" are qualified expenses. The Department of Energy's homeowner guide also counts permitting fees, inspection costs, and developer fees as part of installation labour.
  • Sales tax on eligible expenses, per the Department of Energy's homeowner guide.
  • Also covered under the same credit (same form, different lines): solar water heaters (which must be certified by the Solar Rating Certification Corporation or a comparable state-endorsed body), small wind turbines, and geothermal heat pumps (which must meet the Energy Star requirements in effect at purchase).

What does not count:

  • Interest. "Do not include interest paid including loan origination fees." If you financed the system, the loan costs are not part of your credit base — only the system and installation costs are.
  • The roof itself. "Traditional building components that primarily serve a roofing or structural function generally don't qualify" — roof trusses, decking, and ordinary shingles under your panels earn nothing. Solar roofing tiles and solar shingles do qualify, because they generate the electricity.
  • Costs covered by someone else's money. Public utility subsidies for buying or installing the property must be subtracted from your qualified expenses, whether the subsidy was paid to you or straight to your contractor. Rebates from the seller, manufacturer, or installer that are based on the cost of the property are also subtracted, because they are treated as a reduction in the purchase price. State energy incentives, by contrast, are generally not subtracted unless they qualify as a rebate or purchase-price adjustment under federal tax law. (Payments for power you sell back to the grid, like net metering credits, do not reduce your expenses.)
The Install-Date Trap

The date that counts: installation completed, not money paid

This is where 2025's rush installations get dangerous. In FS-2025-05, the IRS answered the exact question thousands of homeowners asked in December 2025:

"Can a credit be claimed for property installed after December 31, 2025, if a taxpayer pays for the property on or before December 31, 2025?"

The answer is no. "Section 25D(e)(8)(A) provides that an expenditure with respect to an item is treated as made when the original installation of the item is completed. If installation is completed after December 31, 2025, the expenditure will be treated as made after December 31, 2025, which will prevent the taxpayer from claiming the section 25D credit."

There is no grandfather clause for contracts signed in 2025, deposits paid in 2025, or panels delivered in 2025. The completion date is the whole test. (One variant: if the cost was part of constructing or reconstructing a home, the expenditure is treated as made when your original use of the constructed or reconstructed home begins — if that began in 2026, the credit is gone.)

So before anything else, establish your completion date from your paperwork: the final invoice, the passed inspection, or the utility's permission-to-operate notice. If installation was completed on or before December 31, 2025, you are in. If it slipped into January 2026, no filing technique can recover the credit — and any website or installer still quoting you 30% off a 2026 completion is quoting a credit that no longer exists.

Form 5695, Part I

Step by step: claiming it on Form 5695

You claim the credit on Form 5695, Residential Energy Credits, filed with your Form 1040. Solar goes in Part I (Residential Clean Energy Credit). Here is the 2025 form, line by line, for a typical solar-plus-battery installation:

  1. Address block (above line 1). Enter the complete address of the home where the property was installed.
  2. Line 1 — Qualified solar electric property costs. Your total cost for the panels and system, including the allocable labour and wiring described above (after subtracting any utility subsidy or seller rebate).
  3. Lines 2–4. Solar water heating, small wind, and geothermal costs, if you installed any of those in 2025 as well.
  4. Line 5a/5b — Battery storage. Line 5a asks whether the battery has a capacity of at least 3 kWh. If yes, enter the battery costs on line 5b. If the battery is under 3 kWh, its costs cannot be included at all.
  5. Line 6a/6b. Line 6a adds lines 1 through 5b. Line 6b multiplies the total by 30% — that is your gross credit before the fuel-cell lines (7–11, which most solar households skip) and any carryforward.
  6. Line 12 — Carryforward from the prior year. If you had unused credit from a 2024 installation, it enters here (from your 2024 Form 5695, line 16).
  7. Line 13. Adds it all together: your total credit available this year.
  8. Line 14 — The tax-liability limit. A short worksheet in the instructions caps the credit at your tax for the year after certain other credits. This is where "nonrefundable" bites (next section).
  9. Line 15 — Your credit for this year. The smaller of line 13 or line 14. This amount flows to Schedule 3 (Form 1040), line 5a, and from there into your return.
  10. Line 16 — Carryforward to next year. If line 15 is smaller than line 13, the difference is your unused credit, carried forward. Keep this number — it is the starting point of next year's claim.

Tax software walks through the same steps; the line numbers above are how you check its work. If a preparer does your return, hand them the documentation in the checklist below and ask them to confirm the credit appears on Form 5695, Part I — not buried, not skipped.

Worked example (illustrative numbers only). A system completed in November 2025 cost $24,000 all-in, including labour, with no subsidies or rebates. Line 1: $24,000. Line 6b: 30% × $24,000 = $7,200 of credit. If the household's tax-liability limit (line 14) is $5,000, then line 15 is $5,000 — the tax bill drops by $5,000 this year — and line 16 carries $2,200 forward to reduce a future year's tax.

Carryforward Rules

Nonrefundable, and why the carryforward matters

Two rules define what you actually receive:

  • The credit is nonrefundable. In the IRS's words, "the credit amount you receive can't exceed the amount you owe in tax." It can take your federal income tax bill to zero; it cannot, by itself, generate a payment beyond that. (It also never offsets things like self-employment tax — it works against income tax, through the line 14 limit worksheet.)
  • The excess is not lost. "You can carry forward any excess unused credit, though, and apply it to reduce the tax you owe in future years." The Form 5695 instructions add a point people miss: "File this form even if you can't use any of your credit in 2025." Filing the form is what establishes the carryforward on line 16. Skip the form in a low-income year and you have created a paperwork fight for a credit you were entitled to.

There is no dollar cap working against you here — the credit "has no annual or lifetime dollar limit except for credit limits for fuel cell property" — so a large system simply produces a large credit, spread over as many years as your tax bills require.

Filing Routes

Haven't filed yet, already filed, or filing your 2026 return now?

  • Still to file your 2025 return (for example, you took an extension): claim the credit on that return with Form 5695, exactly as above. The IRS rule is to "claim the credit for the tax year when the property is installed, not merely purchased."
  • Already filed your 2025 return and left it off: file an amended return (Form 1040-X) with the Form 5695 attached. Claims for a refund are generally subject to a deadline — usually within three years of the date you filed the original return — so don't leave it sitting.
  • Reading this during the 2026 filing season (early 2027): your 2025 installation does not go on your 2026 return as a new claim. What belongs on the 2026 return is any carryforward — the unused amount from your 2025 Form 5695, line 16 — entered as the carryforward in Part I of that year's Form 5695. And if you never claimed the 2025 credit at all, the fix is amending the 2025 return, not adding it to 2026's.
Record Keeping

Documentation: what to keep

You don't send most of this with your return — you keep it in case the IRS asks. Assemble one folder (paper or digital) containing:

  • ☐ The contract and final, itemised invoice from your installer, showing the total cost and what it covered.
  • ☐ Proof of the completion date — final invoice date, passed inspection sign-off, or the utility's permission-to-operate (PTO) letter. This is the document that proves you beat the December 31, 2025 deadline.
  • ☐ Proof of payment — bank or card records, or the loan documents if financed (remembering that interest itself isn't a qualified cost).
  • ☐ The manufacturer's certification statement for the equipment. Per the Form 5695 instructions, "you can rely on the manufacturer's certification, in writing, that a product is qualifying property for the credit. Don't attach the certification to your return. Keep it for your records." For a battery, keep the spec sheet showing capacity of at least 3 kWh.
  • ☐ Records of any subsidy or rebate you received, so the subtraction from your cost base is documented.
  • ☐ Your filed Form 5695 itself — especially line 16 if you have a carryforward; next year's claim starts from it.

One more record-keeping point from the Form 5695 instructions: if a residential energy credit is allowed for your expenses, "you must reduce the cost basis of your home" by the amount of the credit. Note it in the folder; it matters when you eventually sell.

Avoid These

The common mistakes, collected

  1. Paid in 2025, installed in 2026. No credit. The completion date is the test (FS-2025-05) — the costliest misunderstanding of the whole wind-down.
  2. Claiming it on the wrong year's return. The credit belongs to the year of installation. A 2025 install claimed fresh on a 2026 return is misplaced; the routes are the 2025 return, an amended 2025 return, or the carryforward.
  3. Expecting a refund cheque. Nonrefundable means it can zero out your income tax, no more. The rest carries forward — it doesn't arrive as cash.
  4. Including loan interest or origination fees in the cost base. The IRS says to leave them out.
  5. Claiming the roof. New decking, trusses, or ordinary shingles installed to hold the panels don't qualify; only the energy-generating property (including genuine solar shingles) does.
  6. Forgetting to subtract a utility subsidy or seller rebate — or, the opposite error, subtracting a state incentive that didn't have to be subtracted.
  7. Counting a battery under 3 kWh. Below that capacity, battery costs can't be included at all.
  8. Claiming for a leased system or PPA. If you don't own it, the credit isn't yours.
  9. Claiming used equipment, or a second installation of the same equipment. New property, original installation only.
  10. Not filing Form 5695 in a year you owe little tax. File it anyway — that's what creates your carryforward.
  11. Throwing away the paperwork. The manufacturer's certification and the completion-date proof are the two documents an IRS query will ask for first.
