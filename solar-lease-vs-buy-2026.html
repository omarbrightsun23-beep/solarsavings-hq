Solar Lease vs Buy in 2026: The Answer Flipped

Leasing is now the only route where a federal credit touches a home system at all — but the credit belongs to the provider. Buying still usually wins long-term. Here is the full maths.

Buy Credit: None in 2026
Lease Credit: §48E to Provider
Escalator: Check 0–2.9%/yr
Status: Compare Total Cost

Quick answer: Until 2025, buying won easily, because the buyer claimed the 30% federal tax credit. That credit (Section 25D) expired on December 31, 2025 for new installations. In 2026 a lease or PPA is the only way a federal credit touches a home system at all — the provider that owns the panels can claim the business credit (Section 48E) through 2027. But the credit belongs to the provider, the law does not require any of its value to reach you, and on the published 25-year workings buying still usually ends ahead. The flip is real, but it is narrower than lease sales pitches suggest: leasing now wins on upfront cost and credit access, buying still wins on long-term value and control.

What Changed

What actually changed on January 1, 2026

For a decade, the comparison was lopsided. A homeowner who bought a system claimed the §25D Residential Clean Energy Credit — 30% of the cost — on their own tax return. A homeowner who leased claimed nothing, because they didn't own the equipment. Buying started 30% ahead before a single panel produced anything.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) ended §25D for expenditures after December 31, 2025. The IRS is explicit: the credit "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025," and paying early doesn't help — the expenditure counts when installation is completed.

The same law did not end the business credit. Section 48E, the Clean Electricity Investment Credit, is claimed by whoever owns a generating facility. When a leasing company owns the panels on your roof, it is a business that owns a facility — so it can claim the credit (commonly 30% of the system's cost, subject to the credit's conditions) on systems within the §48E window. For solar facilities whose construction began after July 4, 2026, that generally means the system must be placed in service by December 31, 2027. Energy storage follows a separate, longer schedule.

25-Year Maths

The honest 25-year maths — and why published answers disagree

Every "lease vs buy" page on the internet publishes a 25-year winner, and the totals vary wildly — because the answer depends on your electricity rate, your state's export rules, and the specific escalator in front of you. Here is what fetched competitor workings actually show, with their assumptions, rather than a single invented number:

  • One 2026 comparison modelling an 8 kW system at $2.58/W ($20,640) estimated 25-year net savings of roughly $28,000–$52,000 for a cash purchase depending on state, $12,000–$28,000 for a loan after interest, and only $3,000–$11,000 for a lease.
  • A second 2026 comparison, using a larger 12 kW system at the EnergySage average price ($31,135) and the US average residential rate, estimated 25-year net savings of about $61,800 for cash, $30,500–$41,200 for a loan, and $18,600–$41,500 for a lease/PPA.
  • A California-focused financing guide put an 8 kW system's 25-year net at roughly $48,000 for cash and $15,000–$25,000 for a monthly lease or PPA in SCE territory — while arguing a prepaid lease could beat both in that specific high-rate, low-export-value market.

Three lessons from the spread itself. First, buying ahead is the consistent direction in every published working we fetched — the buy figures we found run about $28,000–$62,000 and the lease figures about $3,000–$41,500, depending heavily on system size and state. Second, the ranges overlap at the edges — a well-priced lease in a high-rate state can beat a badly-priced loan in a cheap-rate state. Third, none of these numbers is your number. They are estimates built on stated assumptions (system price, electricity rate, rate rises, degradation), and yours will differ. Treat any salesperson's single savings figure — for either option — as marketing, and any range with shown assumptions as a starting point.

A worked illustration, with its assumptions on the table. Take an 8 kW system at the EnergySage marketplace average of $2.62/W — $20,960 bought outright (checked November 2026). Assume it produces about 11,400 kWh in year one (a national-average-style yield of roughly 1,400 kWh per kW; your roof will differ — check yours in NREL's free PVWatts calculator). At the US average residential rate of 18.16¢/kWh (EIA, August 2026), that production is worth at most about $2,070 a year if every kilowatt-hour offsets power you would otherwise have bought at retail. On those deliberately simple assumptions — no rate rises, no export discount, no maintenance — the cash system recovers its cost in roughly 10 years and produces for about 15 more. A lease on the same roof might start at, say, $120–$180 a month with nothing upfront: cheaper this year, every year — but you pay it for 20–25 years, it may rise annually (next section), and at the end of the term you own nothing. That is the whole trade, stated plainly: the lease rents you a discount; the purchase buys you the asset.

What Sellers Don't Lead With

The three things sellers of leases don't lead with

1. The escalator clause

Most monthly leases and PPAs raise your payment every year. Escalators in current contracts commonly run 0% to 2.9% a year, and 2.9% compounding is not a small number: one fetched 2026 financing guide works the example of a $180 monthly payment at a 2.9% escalator reaching about $358 a month by year 25 — roughly $77,800 paid over the term instead of $54,000 for the same starting payment with no escalator. That is about $23,800 of extra cost carried by one sentence in the contract.

The deeper trap: your lease payment rises at a guaranteed rate, while your savings depend on utility prices rising at least as fast. If your utility's rates rise 1% a year and your lease rises 2.9%, there comes a year — calculable before you sign — when the lease payment exceeds the value of the power. Ask for three numbers in writing: the year-1 payment, the year-25 payment, and the total of all payments. A provider who won't state them is telling you something.

2. Selling your home gets harder

An owned system conveys with the house; studies aside, it is simply part of the property. A leased system is a contract a buyer must agree to assume — and qualify for — on top of their mortgage, at whatever the escalated payment has reached by then. If the buyer refuses, your options are typically to buy out the remaining contract (a five-figure sum mid-term is not unusual in contract terms) or to pay to have the system removed and the contract settled. Estate agents have flagged leased panels as a sale complication for years; in 2026, with more leases being signed, it deserves to be a first-order part of the decision, not a footnote. If you expect to move within the term — and most lease terms are 20–25 years — price that risk in.

3. The provider does not have to pass the credit to you

This is the fact the 2026 sales pitch most often blurs. Yes, your lease exists partly because the provider can claim §48E. But nothing in the law requires the provider to lower your price by any amount because of that credit. The credit is the provider's income, alongside your monthly payments, any state incentives the contract assigns to them, and often the renewable-energy certificates your system generates. How much of that stack is reflected in your monthly price is a pricing decision by the provider, visible only by comparison: get a cash quote and a loan quote for the same system on the same roof, and compare all three on total cost over the same years. If the lease's total payments approach the purchase price, you are paying ownership money for a rental.

Two related contract points worth one question each: who claims the state incentives (in most states, the system owner — the provider; New York is a notable exception, where a lease/PPA customer on a term of at least 10 years can claim the state credit of 25% of lease payments, capped at $5,000), and what happens at the end of the term — renewal at a new price, purchase at a stated or "fair market" value, or removal, and who pays for removal and roof repair.

When Leasing Makes Sense

When leasing genuinely makes sense in 2026

Neutral means printing this side too. A lease or PPA can be the right choice when:

  • You cannot use a purchase well — no cash and no appetite for a loan, but a high electricity bill today. A fairly-priced lease with a 0% escalator still cuts a bill with $0 down.
  • You expect to move soon and the transfer terms are genuinely simple — read them, don't assume them; a short remaining term transfers more easily than a fresh 25-year one.
  • Your roof or usage makes ownership maths weak — heavy shade, low usage, or a low export rate can make a right-sized PPA's guaranteed per-kWh price the cleaner deal.
  • You value the provider carrying maintenance and production risk — if the system underproduces under a PPA, you pay for what it made; under ownership, the shortfall is yours (subject to any production guarantee you negotiated).
  • You are in California under NEM 3.0 or a similar low-export-value regime, where the value of owning the exported power has fallen — several 2026 guides argue prepaid or well-structured leases compete hardest exactly there. Our California page walks through the state rules.

And buying makes sense when: you have cash or a fairly-priced loan (watch for dealer fees rolled into financed prices — Berkeley Lab's 2025 data shows loan-financed systems at a median $4.50/W against $3.00/W for cash), you plan to stay put, your rate is high, and your state still pays something — see the Texas page for how buyback-plan choice changes the Texas maths entirely.
