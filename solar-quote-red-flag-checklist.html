My Solar Quote Still Includes the 30% Tax Credit — Is It Outdated? The Red-Flag Checklist

A 2026 quote that subtracts 30% as a federal credit is using maths that no longer exists. Check that line first — then $/W, production claims and the other flags below — before you sign.

Credit Line: Check First
Benchmark: $2.60–$3.60/W
Production: PVWatts Check
Status: 10 Flags Listed

Quick answer: Yes — if the quote is for a system that will be installed in 2026. The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D, 30% of cost) expired on December 31, 2025, and it does not apply to any installation completed after that date. A 2026 quote that subtracts 30% as a "federal tax credit" is using maths that no longer exists, and the price you will actually pay is the higher number above that line. That one line is the biggest red flag on this page — but it is not the only one worth checking before you sign.

Why This Is Happening

Why this is happening in 2026

The 30% credit was scheduled to run through 2032. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025) cut it short. The IRS states the result plainly: the §25D credit "will not be allowed for any expenditures made after December 31, 2025," and it "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025."

The completion date is the test, not the payment date. The IRS treats an expenditure as made when the original installation is completed. Paying in full in 2025 for a system finished in January 2026 earned no credit — and a system finished in 2026 earns none either, whatever the contract date says.

There is one legitimate way a federal credit can still appear in a 2026 offer — and it is not on a purchase quote. If you lease the system or buy its power under a PPA, the company that owns the equipment may claim a separate business credit (Section 48E). That credit belongs to the company, not to you, and it should never appear as a line subtracting 30% from your purchase price. If a purchase quote shows it, the quote is wrong. (Our companion article, Solar lease vs buy in 2026, explains that side in full.)

Everything else on a 2026 purchase quote — state credits, utility rebates, net-metering savings — may be real. The checklist below separates those from the lines that should stop you signing.

The Checklist

The red-flag checklist

Work down the list with your quote in hand. One flag does not automatically mean a bad installer — templates lag, and honest companies made the same template error in early 2026. Two or more flags, or any flag the salesperson cannot explain in writing, means get another quote before you sign anything.

Red Flag 1

☐ Red flag 1: A §25D / "30% federal tax credit" line on a 2026 installation

The headline check. Look for any of these wordings: "Federal ITC −30%", "Federal tax credit −$X", "Net cost after federal incentives" on a cash or loan purchase.

The test: ask one question — "Will my installation be completed in 2026?" If yes, that line must come out, and the honest price is the gross price. On an $24,000 quote, a phantom 30% line hides $7,200 you will actually pay.

What is not a flag: a state credit line (a handful of states still pay one — New York, for example, pays 25% of cost up to $5,000), a utility rebate, or a lease/PPA quote where the provider's credit is mentioned as the reason the monthly price is what it is. Those are different money. The flag is specifically a homeowner §25D credit subtracted from a 2026 purchase.

Red Flag 2

☐ Red flag 2: Price per watt far above the published benchmarks

Divide the quote's total price (before any incentives) by the system size in watts. An 8 kW system is 8,000 watts, so a $32,000 quote is $32,000 ÷ 8,000 = $4.00 per watt. Now compare it with the three public benchmarks (all checked November 2026, all before incentives):

The benchmarks disagree with each other by about $1/W because they measure different things — marketplace quotes run low, recorded payments run high. That disagreement is exactly why the check works as a range: a quote inside roughly $2.60–$3.60/W is within the published evidence. A quote well above $3.60/W needs a written explanation (steep or complex roof, main-panel upgrade, long trench run, battery included in the headline price). A quote at $4.50/W or more with no such explanation is, on the published evidence, simply expensive. Our full breakdown is in Solar panel cost in 2026.

One more split inside the Berkeley Lab data worth knowing: in 2025, cash buyers paid a median of $3.00/W while loan-financed buyers paid $4.50/W — because lender "dealer fees" are commonly rolled into a financed system's price. If your quote is financed, compare it against the financed reality, and ask for the cash price of the same system in writing. The gap between the two is the dealer's fee, made visible.

Red Flag 3

☐ Red flag 3: Production claims you cannot reproduce

Every quote promises an annual production figure in kilowatt-hours, and the savings maths is built on it. Check it against NREL's free PVWatts calculator (pvwatts.nrel.gov), which models production for your address, roof tilt and direction from satellite weather data.

The quick sanity check, before you even open PVWatts: divide the claimed production by the system size. Across most of the United States, 1 kW of south-facing panels produces roughly 1,100–1,900 kWh a year depending on location, with a national-average figure around 1,400 kWh per kW. So an 8 kW system typically produces somewhere near 9,000–14,500 kWh in its first year. A quote claiming 16,000 kWh from 8 kW (2,000 kWh per kW) is claiming production beyond what the sun delivers almost anywhere in the country — and every savings figure downstream of it is inflated by the same percentage.

Then run PVWatts itself with your real tilt and azimuth. If the quote's number is more than about 10% above the PVWatts estimate for the same roof, ask why, in writing. Legitimate reasons exist (a ground mount at ideal tilt, a sunnier microclimate) — but "our panels are more efficient" is not one of them; efficiency changes how much roof a system needs, not how much sun falls on it.

Red Flag 4

☐ Red flag 4: Savings built on your whole bill disappearing

A system only saves you the value of the electricity it produces and that you use or get credited for. Quotes that assume 100% of production offsets your full retail rate overstate savings wherever exports are paid less than retail — which, after California's NEM 3.0 and similar changes elsewhere, is much of the country. Check three things: the export/buyback rate the quote assumes, whether it assumes your usage stays flat for 25 years, and whether the "25-year savings" headline quietly assumes electricity prices rise 4–5% a year forever. At the US average residential rate of 18.16¢/kWh (EIA, August 2026), an 8 kW system producing about 11,400 kWh a year is worth at most about $2,070 a year if every kilowatt-hour offsets a retail purchase — any first-year savings claim far above the value of the production at your actual rate deserves a line-by-line explanation.

Red Flag 5

☐ Red flag 5: Vague equipment — "premium panels" with no model numbers

A real quote names the panel manufacturer and model, the panel wattage and count, the inverter type (string, microinverters, or optimisers) and model, and the battery brand and capacity if one is included. "Tier 1 panels, 25-year warranty" with no model numbers means the installer can substitute whatever is cheapest at install time — and it makes your $/W comparison meaningless, because you cannot tell a 400 W panel system from a 430 W one. No model numbers, no signature.

Red Flag 6

☐ Red flag 6: The escalator hiding inside a financed or lease quote

If any part of the offer is a lease or PPA, find the escalator: the annual percentage rise in your payment. A common 2.9% escalator turns a $180 monthly payment into about $358 a month by year 25, and roughly $77,800 in total payments over 25 years instead of $54,000 for the same payment with no escalator. If the escalator is not stated on the summary page, it is in the contract's fine print — ask for the year-25 payment in writing. On a loan quote, the equivalent trap is the dealer fee from Red flag 2: ask for the total of all payments over the loan term, not just the monthly figure.

Red Flag 7

☐ Red flag 7: Pressure with a deadline that does not exist

"The credit expires Friday" was a real deadline in December 2025. In 2026 there is no federal deadline for a homeowner purchase at all — the credit is already gone — and the genuine deadline that does exist (leased/PPA systems generally must be placed in service by December 31, 2027 for the provider's §48E credit) is more than a year away. Same-day-signature discounts, "this price is only good while I'm at your house," and refusal to leave a written quote behind are sales techniques, not market facts. A price that cannot survive 48 hours of checking was not a real price.

Red Flag 8

☐ Red flag 8: No site-specific design — a quote written without your bill or your roof

A credible quote is built from your last 12 months of electricity use (in kWh, from your bills), a roof assessment (age, shading, direction), and your utility's current buyback rules. If the proposal was generated from your address alone, sizes the system to your roof's maximum rather than your usage, or never asked for a bill, the production and savings figures are template numbers. Oversizing is the common failure: panels that export at a few cents per kWh what you paid retail-rate money to generate.

Red Flag 9

☐ Red flag 9: Permits, interconnection and roof work listed as "extra" later

The benchmarks above are for complete, installed systems. If the quote's headline price excludes permitting, utility interconnection, a main electrical panel upgrade your home plainly needs, or removing and reinstalling panels when the roof is replaced, the real $/W is higher than the headline. Ask one question: "What is the total I will have paid when the system is switched on, including everything on this list?" — and get the answer as a single number in writing.

Red Flag 10

☐ Red flag 10: No production guarantee, or a warranty you cannot read

Most reputable quotes state who warrants what: panels (commonly 25-year performance), inverter, workmanship, and roof penetrations. Vague warranty language, a workmanship warranty from a company formed last year, or no stated remedy if the system underproduces, are all flags — not because every young installer is bad, but because a 25-year promise is only worth the company standing behind it.

Worked Example

Worked example: one quote, three checks

A homeowner sends us the shape of a real 2026 quote (numbers rounded, details typical):

  • 8 kW system, gross price $32,000, with a line reading "Federal tax credit (30%) −$9,600" and a "net cost" of $22,400
  • Claimed production: 15,200 kWh/year; claimed first-year savings: $3,100

Check 1 — the credit line. Installation would complete in 2026. §25D does not apply. The real price is $32,000, not $22,400. Flag confirmed.

Check 2 — price per watt. $32,000 ÷ 8,000 W = $4.00/W. That is $1.38/W above the EnergySage 8 kW average ($2.62/W, $20,960) and $0.40/W above even the Berkeley Lab all-payments median ($3.60/W, $28,800). Unexplained, this quote is roughly $11,000 above the marketplace average for the same size. Flag confirmed.

Check 3 — production. 15,200 ÷ 8 = 1,900 kWh per kW — the very top of the US range, achievable in the sunniest desert sites on an ideal roof. For most addresses PVWatts will return materially less; at the national-average ~1,400 kWh/kW the same system makes about 11,400 kWh, worth about $2,070 in year one at the EIA average rate — not $3,100. Flag confirmed.

Three flags, one conclusion: do not sign this quote. Get two competing quotes for the same 8 kW, compare all three on $/W and PVWatts-checked production, and let the first installer explain the gaps in writing if they want the job.
