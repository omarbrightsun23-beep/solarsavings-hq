Is There Still a Solar Tax Credit in 2026? What Ended, What Still Pays

The homeowner credit ended for systems completed in 2026 — completion, not payment, is the test. State credits, SRECs, net metering and the provider's §48E credit are what remain.

Homeowner Credit: $0 in 2026
§25D Ended: Dec 31, 2025
2025 Installs: Still Claimable
§48E (Lease/PPA): Through 2027

Quick answer: No — not for a homeowner who buys a system completed in 2026. The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit under Section 25D, worth 30% of the cost, ended for installations completed after 31 December 2025. If your system was finished in 2025, you can still claim the 30% credit on your 2025 tax return. If it is finished in 2026, there is no federal credit to claim, however early you signed or paid. What still pays in 2026 is different money: state credits in some states, solar renewable energy certificates (SRECs) where a market exists, net metering value, and — on a lease or power purchase agreement (PPA) — a separate business credit claimed by the company that owns the system, not by you.

What Ended

What ended: the §25D credit, and the exact date that matters

For more than a decade, the centre of almost every home solar quote was one line: a federal tax credit worth 30% of the installed cost. Under the Inflation Reduction Act, that credit — the Residential Clean Energy Credit, Section 25D — was scheduled to continue at 30% into the early 2030s. Many articles, calculators and templates still describe that schedule. It is no longer the law.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21), enacted in 2025, brought the end date forward sharply. The IRS states the new position in two places, and both agree. Its guidance on Public Law 119-21 says the §25D credit "will not be allowed for any expenditures made after December 31, 2025." Its Residential Clean Energy Credit page says the credit equals 30% of the cost of property installed from 2022 through 31 December 2025, and "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025."

Almost every misunderstanding comes from one word in that sentence: expenditures. When is an expenditure treated as made?

Completion Rule

The rule is completion, not payment

The IRS answers directly in its FAQs on Public Law 119-21. Under Section 25D(e)(8)(A), an expenditure is treated as made when the original installation is completed. So if you paid on or before 31 December 2025 but installation was completed after that date, the expenditure counts as made after the cut-off — and the credit cannot be claimed.

Three common beliefs fail against that rule:

  • "I signed in 2025." The contract date does not fix the credit. Completion does.
  • "I paid in full in 2025." Payment does not fix it either. Paid in 2025, finished in January 2026 = no §25D credit.
  • "My installer said it was locked in." Unless installation was completed by 31 December 2025, there was nothing to lock in. The IRS guidance contains no grandfather clause for signed or paid-for projects.

The reverse is also true: a system completed in 2025 qualifies even though you file for it in 2026. You claim it on your 2025 tax return, using Form 5695, Residential Energy Credits — see How to claim the solar tax credit for a 2025 install.

One more point protects people who did qualify: the credit is nonrefundable — it cannot reduce your tax below zero in the claim year — but any unused amount carries forward to later years, with no expiry in the IRS guidance. Keep your Form 5695 and completion-date evidence with your tax records.

The Four Cases

The four cases: which one are you?

Most confusion disappears once you place yourself in one row of this table, by completion date and who owns the system.

CaseYour situationFederal credit in 2026?
1System installed and completed in 2025; you own itYes — §25D at 30%. Claim on your 2025 return via Form 5695. Unused credit carries forward with no expiry.
2Paid or contracted in 2025, but installation completed in 2026; you own itNo §25D. The expenditure counts as made when installation was completed — in 2026, after the cut-off.
3Buying in 2026 as the homeowner-ownerNo federal credit. Price any quote on its gross cost; a "net after 30% credit" figure does not apply to you.
4Lease or PPA in 2026 — a company owns the systemYou claim nothing personally. The owner company may claim the §48E business credit (available through 2027) and may reflect its value in your pricing.

Case 2 is the painful one. The deciding document is not your payment receipt; it is evidence of when installation was completed. If you believe your system was in fact completed in 2025, gather the paperwork that shows it — the installer's completion record and inspection or permission-to-operate documents can matter. If completion genuinely fell in 2026, no filing position recreates the credit, and any adviser who suggests backdating paperwork is one to walk away from. Case 4 — the one place a federal credit legitimately appears in 2026 — is covered below.

What Still Pays

What still pays in 2026

The end of §25D is not the end of solar economics. It is the end of one large, simple subsidy, and what remains is a patchwork — smaller, more local, and more dependent on where you live and how you buy. Four sources of value are still real in 2026.

State Credits

1. State credits and state incentives

A handful of states run their own solar credits, entirely separate from federal law, and Public Law 119-21 did not touch them. Two clear examples, checked November 2026:

  • New York offers a state solar tax credit of 25% of the cost, up to $5,000, for a qualifying home system, alongside its NY-Sun incentive programme.
  • Massachusetts offers a state solar tax credit of 15% of the cost, up to $1,000.

Treat those figures as a starting point, not a promise. State programmes have their own eligibility rules, caps and forms, they change on state budgets, and most states offer no credit at all. Check your own state's energy office or tax department for the year your system is completed — a real state credit always has a name, a form and a cap, and an installer should name all three.

SRECs

2. SRECs, where a market exists

Where a solar renewable energy certificate market exists, each megawatt-hour your system generates can create a certificate that utilities or suppliers buy to meet state requirements. SREC income is real money where an active market exists, but it is variable: prices move, programmes close to new entrants, and many states have no market at all. If a quote includes SREC income, ask which programme, at what assumed price, and for how many years — then check the programme's own published prices, not the quote's.

Net Metering

3. Net metering and export value

The longest-running source of solar value is the electricity itself: every kilowatt-hour your panels produce and your home uses is one you do not buy at retail. What you are credited for exports varies far more — some utilities still credit close to the retail rate under net metering, others pay much less, and that gap is now a major variable in payback. A quote should state the export arrangement it assumes for your utility; if it assumes every exported kilowatt-hour is worth your full retail rate, its savings figures are probably too high.

Section 48E

4. The §48E business credit — through a lease, PPA or prepaid arrangement

Section 48E is a federal business credit for clean electricity investment. It belongs to whoever owns the equipment — which is why a homeowner can benefit from it only indirectly:

  • On a lease, the leasing company owns the system, may claim §48E, and can pass some value through in a lower monthly payment.
  • On a PPA, the provider owns the system, may claim §48E, and can pass value through in its per-kilowatt-hour price.
  • On some prepaid arrangements, part of the value is reflected in the upfront price instead.

Two cautions matter more than the headline. First, you do not claim §48E yourself — it will never appear on your personal Form 5695, and any document suggesting you personally receive a federal credit from a 2026 lease or PPA is misstating the arrangement. Second, the law does not require the owner company to pass the credit's value to you at all, so judge a lease or PPA on its own numbers — monthly payment, escalator, total paid over the term — against the gross price of owning. See Solar lease vs buy in 2026. The §48E route is also time-limited — available through 2027 for qualifying systems on the current law — a real deadline for providers, not a reason to be rushed into signing this week.

Warning

Warning: how to spot a quote or article still using the dead credit

Because §25D anchored solar marketing for so long, its ghost is everywhere in 2026: installer templates never updated, calculators still subtracting 30% by default, and pre-2025 articles still ranking for exactly the question this page answers. You need one test to spot it.

The test: find the completion year, then find the credit line. If the system will be completed in 2026 and the price presentation subtracts a federal credit, the presentation is wrong. Wordings to watch for:

  • "Net cost after 30% federal tax credit" on a 2026 purchase quote
  • "Federal ITC −30%" or "Federal tax credit −$" reducing your purchase price
  • A calculator or article that applies 30% automatically, with no completion-date question
  • Savings or payback figures that only work if the net (after-credit) price is real

The error is not small. On a $30,000 system, a phantom 30% line hides $9,000 — the difference between the $21,000 "net cost" on the page and the $30,000 you will actually pay. Every payback figure built on the lower number is wrong by the same proportion.

Not red flags, so you do not overcorrect: a state credit line naming the state programme (such as New York's above); a lease or PPA document explaining that the provider's pricing reflects the provider's business credit; and anything about claiming for a 2025 completion on a 2025 return — that credit is alive for that purpose (2025 claim guide).

If you find the dead credit in a quote, ask the installer to reissue it at the gross price, with any genuine state incentive named separately, and to confirm in writing that no federal credit applies to a 2026 completion. An honest company will do this without argument — template lag was common in early 2026. A company that argues, or re-labels the same 30% as something vaguer, has told you what you need to know. Still Worth It?

So is solar still worth it in 2026?

For some households, yes; for others, no — 2026 removed the subsidy that used to paper over that difference. The case now rests on four numbers, none of them a federal credit: the gross price per watt quoted, your retail electricity rate, how much of your production you use yourself rather than export, and any state incentive or SREC income that genuinely applies at your address. A well-priced system on a high-rate utility, sized to your own usage, can still pay back in a reasonable period. An expensive system on a low rate, justified by a credit that no longer exists, cannot.
