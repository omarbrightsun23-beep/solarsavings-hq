Is Solar Still Worth It in New Jersey, Massachusetts and New York Without the Federal Credit? (2026 Payback)

Worked gross-cost paybacks for all three states — about 6.4–7.5 years in New Jersey, 6.8–8.0 in Massachusetts before SMART, and 5.9–7.2 in New York before NY-Sun.

Quick answer: Yes — in all three states, on the gross-cost maths in this article, but by a narrower margin than the old tables suggest, and not for every house. With no federal credit at all, a typical system in our worked examples pays back in about 6.4–7.5 years in New Jersey, 6.8–8.0 years in Massachusetts (before any SMART payment), and 5.9–7.2 years in New York (before any NY-Sun payment). The reason these three states still work is not sunshine. It is price: all three have some of the highest residential electricity rates in the country, and each still pays a real state incentive. Remove the credit honestly and the case survives here — which is exactly why it is worth doing the maths without it.

Why Tables Are Wrong

Why most payback tables you will find are wrong in 2026

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) ended for installations completed after December 31, 2025. For a system completed in 2026, the federal credit is $0 — not reduced, not phased down, gone. Our companion article, Is there still a solar tax credit in 2026?, sets out the position in full.

Yet many state payback tables still ranking for this question were built while the 30% credit existed, and some still subtract it — in places, on the same page that reports the credit has ended. On a $22,000 system, a phantom 30% credit understates the price you will actually pay by $6,600, and shortens every payback by roughly three years. If a table does not state whether its prices are gross or net of a federal credit, treat its payback as unverified.

This article does the opposite. Every cost below is the gross cost — the full installed price, before any incentive. The only deductions are state incentives that still exist in 2026, named by programme, and each one is flagged as a programme to verify before you buy, because programmes change, fill up and close. Nothing federal is subtracted anywhere.

The Method

The method, and the numbers it rests on

Simple payback here means one thing: the cost after state incentives only, divided by the first-year value of what the system produces. It ignores degradation, maintenance, roof work, financing cost and future price rises, in both directions. It is a screening number, not a forecast.

These are statewide averages — revenue divided by kilowatt-hours sold, fixed charges included — not the tariff on any one bill. Your rate may sit above or below it, which is why each state section also gives a sensitivity line at prices 20% lower and 20% higher. Use the rate on your own bill before you act on any of this.

Production. The worked examples use conservative yields of roughly 1,150–1,220 kWh per kW per year — the range a well-sited, largely unshaded roof in these states typically models at in NREL's PVWatts. A shaded or north-facing roof will produce less, and every payback stretches in proportion. Run PVWatts for your own address before trusting any production figure, ours included.

A note on what is not counted. Where a programme's payment depends on your utility, system or a current funding round — Massachusetts SMART and New York NY-Sun — this article does not invent a dollar figure for it. Those paybacks are deliberately conservative: a real programme payment, if you qualify, can only shorten them. Verify eligibility and current terms at DSIRE (dsireusa.org) and with your state public utilities commission before purchase.

New Jersey

New Jersey: net metering and the ADI payment carry the case

New Jersey combines two things that matter more than sunshine. First, 1:1 net metering: a kilowatt-hour you export offsets one you buy, at the full retail rate, for systems covered by the state's net-metering rules. Second, the ADI programme (Administratively Determined Incentive, part of the Successor Solar Incentive programme) pays $85.90 per MWh of production — one SREC-II-style certificate per megawatt-hour — for 15 years. Both figures are from the 2026-11-26 research report; verify the current ADI value for your utility and project type before purchase, because values can change for new registrants.

The ADI payment does not move with the electricity price, which is part of why the New Jersey range stays comparatively tight.

Who should still wait in New Jersey: households with a shaded or mainly north-facing roof, where production — and with it both the bill savings and the per-MWh ADI payment — falls together; anyone planning to move within about five years; and anyone whose roof needs replacing soon, since removing and refitting panels later is a real cost none of the figures above include. If your quote sits well above $3.03/W with no written explanation, getting a competing quote is the cheapest improvement available.

Massachusetts

Massachusetts: the highest rate in this comparison does the heavy lifting

Massachusetts had the highest average residential price of these three states in August 2026 — 30.48¢/kWh (EIA, August 2026) — about 68% above the U.S. average. That price, more than any incentive, is what keeps the Massachusetts case alive without a federal credit.

The state incentives are real but smaller. Massachusetts pays a state income tax credit of 15% of cost, up to $1,000. It also runs the SMART programme (Solar Massachusetts Renewable Target), a per-kWh production incentive — but the SMART rate depends on your utility, system size, adder eligibility and the current capacity block, and blocks fill. The research behind this article confirms the programme, not a single rate we can responsibly print for every reader. So the worked example counts the $1,000 credit and excludes SMART entirely. Treat the payback as a ceiling: a SMART payment you qualify for will shorten it.

Massachusetts has the widest sensitivity range of the three states, for a simple reason: with no per-MWh state payment counted, almost all of its value rides on the electricity price itself.

Who should still wait in Massachusetts: anyone whose payback only works at a SMART rate they have not had confirmed in writing for their utility and capacity block; households on a materially cheaper municipal light plant tariff, whose savings may sit far below the state average; and anyone with heavy shade or a roof near the end of its life. At a 20%-lower electricity price, the pre-SMART payback reaches a decade — a household in that position is right to wait for a better quote or a confirmed SMART rate.

New York

New York: the state credit is the largest single deduction left in the country

New York is the one state here where a state credit still does serious work: an income tax credit of 25% of system cost, up to $5,000. Alongside it, the NY-Sun programme pays incentives through participating installers; the amount varies by region, sector and current funding, so — as with SMART — this article does not assign it an invented dollar figure. New York also locks 1:1 net metering for 20 years for residential systems covered by its rules, protecting the value of exported power across most of a system's early life. Verify eligibility, the credit's carryover rules and the current NY-Sun incentive with NYSERDA, DSIRE and the Public Service Commission before purchase.

Note the cap: at the higher benchmark price, 25% of cost would be about $5,681, but the credit stops at $5,000. The more expensive your quote, the smaller the share the credit covers — one more reason the $/W check matters more in New York, not less.

Sensitivity: at an electricity price 20% lower (22.92¢/kWh), first-year savings fall to about $1,977 and payback stretches to roughly 7.4–9.0 years; at 20% higher (34.38¢/kWh), savings rise to about $2,965 and payback shortens to roughly 4.9–6.0 years — in both cases before any NY-Sun payment, which would improve every one of those figures.

Who should still wait in New York: households without enough state tax liability to use a non-refundable credit promptly — check the carryover position with a tax adviser before assuming the full $5,000 lands in year one; New York City households where roof rights, co-op or condo approval, or landmark rules block an installable roof; and anyone quoted well above benchmark, where the capped credit covers a shrinking share of an inflated price.
